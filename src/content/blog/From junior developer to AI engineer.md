Four months ago I joined a logistics company as a junior developer. Today I work as a logistics software and AI engineer. The biggest change wasn't my title. It was how I work. Here's what that shift looked like and what I learned along the way.

## Month one: learning the systems

My first month was about learning the systems, the departments, and where I fit in. I started out fixing bugs and improving the UI, with very little backend work.

I used AI the way a lot of developers do at first. Sometimes I'd ask it to find a bug and then fix it myself. Other times I'd find the bug, track down where it lived, and ask an AI assistant to help with the fix.

I took pride in doing things by hand. Honestly, I sometimes held back from using AI at all, because I worried people would look down on me for it.

## Going AI-first

About a month in, our leadership set a clear direction: we would be an AI-first company and use these tools to their full potential.

My first question was how much more one person could really get done. I understand giving 100%. How does anyone double or triple their output?

So I started leaning in. I used AI more heavily and began learning about agentic workflows, skills, and how to use the context an AI builds up over time. Newer, more capable models were arriving around then, and they made this kind of work much more practical. I went deep on agentic workflows, AI harnesses, and prompting.

## From prompts to workflows

As my responsibilities grew, one-off prompts stopped being enough. I needed AI to work alongside me across several systems, not just answer questions one at a time.

So I built a setup that connects AI agents to the tools I use every day, so they can help with real engineering work from start to finish, with me reviewing and approving what ships.

That's when the question from month one got its answer. With the right workflow, one developer really can do the work of several.

## A setup that knows how I work

My day now runs on:

- **Daily agendas**, so I start each morning knowing what needs attention.
- **Automated ticketing**, so issues get captured and organized without me filing each one by hand.
- **Memory built from working across our systems**, so my setup knows my coding principles, my approach to unit testing, and the standard I expect from my code.

It doesn't replace my judgment. It carries my standards forward so I don't have to restate them every time, and I can focus on the hard problems.

## What I've shipped

In four months I've:

- Built several end-to-end applications.
- Supported multiple production applications with bug fixes, features, and improvements.
- Helped our IT team with an inventory system and a plan to roll it out.
- Written useful scripts in minutes instead of hours or even days.
- Helped plan upcoming AI projects with my team.

## What I learned

1. **Using AI isn't a shortcut to be embarrassed about.** My hesitation early on cost me time. These tools are part of the job now.
2. **Prompts are a start. Workflows are what scale.** Going from asking for help with one bug to workflows that handle my agenda and tickets is where the real gains came from.
3. **Context is everything.** A setup that remembers your systems and your standards is worth more than a smarter model with no memory.
4. **Growth comes from taking on more.** Every time my responsibilities grew, I learned faster.

If you're a junior developer still on the fence about leaning into AI, don't wait for permission. Start small, keep your standards high, and build the workflow that lets you do more.
